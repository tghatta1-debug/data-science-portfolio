Project 1 · Exploratory data analysis

Cost and completion at North Carolina public universities

Research question: How do average net price and six-year graduation rates compare among public four-year universities in North Carolina?

Why this matters

College affordability and student completion are closely related to the choices students and families make. This project compares institutional-level measures to describe variation across North Carolina's public four-year universities.

Data and variables

The U.S. Department of Education College Scorecard API supplied 16 institutional records after filtering to North Carolina public institutions where a bachelor's degree is the predominant award. Each row represents one institution. Average net price is the annual cost of attendance minus grant and scholarship aid for the reported student group; it is operationalized here as the College Scorecard public-institution net-price field. Completion is operationalized as the overall six-year graduation-rate field. Student size is included to scale the scatter-plot markers.

Preparation

All 16 filtered records contained values for the two analysis variables and student size, so no rows were removed for missing data. Graduation rates were converted from proportions to percentages. This produces a comparable institutional dataset, but it does not make the measures causal or student-level.

16 institutions analyzed

61.0% average graduation rate

Limitations and ethics

Institutional averages conceal differences among students and programs. Net price is not the same as a student's actual out-of-pocket cost, and graduation rates may not capture transfer students or part-time pathways. The project avoids ranking institutions as simply better or worse and treats the findings as descriptive.

Conclusion

The data show meaningful variation in both cost and completion. A higher net price alone cannot explain a graduation rate. A next step would include student demographics, aid, retention, and program-level outcomes to better understand the differences.

Introductory reflection

What I am learning through this portfolio.

I am building this portfolio as a place to show how I am developing as a student and as someone interested in data, technology, and business. My first goal is to learn how to take a broad question and make it specific enough to investigate. The College Scorecard project helped me practice that process. Rather than trying to decide which university is best, I focused on a narrower comparison: how average net price and six-year graduation rates differ among North Carolina public four-year institutions. That choice made it possible to use public data, write a clear question, and explain the limits of the answer.

The project also taught me that working with data is not only about making charts. Before creating the visualizations, I had to decide what one row represented, which institutions belonged in the comparison, what the variables meant, and how to handle missing values. Those choices affect the story the data can tell. I learned to be careful about conclusions: a relationship between cost and graduation rate does not prove that one causes the other. Factors such as admissions, student support, student preparation, and program mix can influence both.

As I continue studying Data Science and Business Administration, I want to improve my technical skills while keeping the communication side of analysis in mind. I am interested in work where data supports practical decisions and where results can be explained clearly to people who are not specialists. This portfolio will grow as I complete more coursework and projects, but this first analysis gives me a starting point that I can discuss honestly and build on.
